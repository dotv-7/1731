From Low Tide #1 (14-03-2024), repeated in #6, #9 and #12:

Line one — the "as of" date inside the document, not the publication date. Subtract it from today: 12 days is normal, 25 means someone chose not to publish on time, 40 means stop reading and go look at the chain. An attestation is a snapshot of one moment, so the gap tells you whether the snapshot is of the moment the issuer claims.

Line two — the custodian signature. A custodian named inside the issuer's own PDF is a claim; a letter on the custodian's letterhead confirming the balance is a counterparty putting its name on that claim. Spelling of the custodian's name counts, because a misspelling means someone retyped the document.

Line three — the liability line, the one almost nobody reads. Reserves are the asset side, and reserves without liabilities is theatre: a photo of a full vault with no mention of how many people hold a key.

Why those three: each one tests something the headline ratio cannot fake — whether the numbers are current, whether a second party stands behind them, and whether the tokens those assets back are actually counted. Everything else in the PDF is decoration until those hold.

As Salt puts it in #1: the three lines catch sloppiness and omission, not a competent liar. For that you need the chain.

The date, the custodian signature, and the liabilities.

Date: not when it was published — the "as of" date inside, the day the accountant actually looked at the balances. Twelve days stale is normal for a monthly report. Twenty-five means someone chose not to publish on time. Forty, stop reading and go look at the chain.

Liabilities: the line almost nobody reads. How many tokens are outstanding, across every chain, including minted-but-not-yet-issued and anything sitting in the issuer's own treasury, plus anything else payable out of the same pool. Reserves without supply is a photo of a full vault with no mention of how many people hold a key.

Four things, in order, from the custodian section rather than the header.

First, what kind of claim it is. A custodian named in a table inside the issuer's PDF is a claim by the issuer. A letter, on the custodian's own letterhead, with a signature and a date, is a claim by the custodian. Different claims, different people on the hook. So count signatures, not names — a table can list five custodians and evince zero of them.

Second, whether the document plausibly came from the custodian at all. Custodian PDFs are generated from the custodian's own templates, and custodians do not misspell their own legal name. "Pellican" on one page and "Pelican" on another, or "Pelican Custody Services" where the legal name has no "Services", means someone at the issuer retyped it — and then the whole file stops being signed, whatever the letterhead says.

Third, what the signature actually covers. A signed letter that confirms "a balance" without naming the share of reserves it accounts for has not signed off on the total. That is Meridian's gap in issue #9: the Pelican letter was real and still did not say what portion it covered, and that omission is the issuer's, not Pelican's. So add the signed custodian lines and see whether they sum to the reserve total. An unsigned "other qualified custodians" residual under 5% is housekeeping; at 27% it is the attestation telling you about the part it can prove and hoping you will not ask about the rest.

Fourth, what the custodian says when asked. She emails the listed press address and prints whatever comes back. "We do not comment on client relationships" is not a confirmation — and, as she put it in issue #8, she was wrong to log it as neutral: a custodian that genuinely produced a document can simply say so, so a non-confirmation from the named custodian is itself a finding.
